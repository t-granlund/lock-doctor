The Lock Doctor, the Apprentice,
and the Third Set of Hands

A locksmith fixed my car. An agent built his package. And both of them hit the same wall — the one this story is about removing.

The morning

2022 Bronco Sport. All keys lost — not a copy job, a full originate. Greg Luka showed up with his apprentice, a Ford Transit, and a tool chest that means business. About an hour later: new blade cut, new fob programmed, every old key erased from the car's memory so nothing lost could ever start it again. No drama, no permission slip. Just ownership, done at the machine.

While they worked, a third set of hands was busy. By the time the van pulled out, there was a dossier, a full business analysis of Greg's shop, and a presentation — researched, written, designed, and delivered. All built by one agent, in one voice, from one driveway. That part matters, and I'll come back to it.

The wall

Here's the honest part. They were excited — genuinely. And then came the question that every builder should learn to love, because it's never going away:

“This is cool. But… how does this help us?”the most honest question in the building

I'd heard it before, almost word for word, from a muralist couple months back. Talent, hustle, real work — and a wall between them and the tools. It's not a intelligence gap and it's certainly not a effort gap. It's intimidation. The technology has been made to feel like it belongs to other people. That wall is a design failure, and it's ours to remove, not theirs to climb.

The key that started it

Greg told the origin himself: Lock Doctor exists because he locked himself out of his own house once — and instead of calling somebody, he wanted to figure out how the lock worked. That curiosity became a 22-year trade. And look what the key became along the way: a blade became a fob; a fob became a digital pass key riding in a phone wallet; a car now trusts a VIN over the air. Keys and access keep changing shape — and Greg keeps being the person who can still open the door.

That's the part worth seeing plainly: keys and access have always been the business. The grove is simply the next key — access to your own tools. It removes the distance between you and the vendors, the dealers, the paperwork. And where the vendors leave a gap, you can employ yourself: build your own small tools, automate the annoying middle, open paths of your own.

The plain answer

So here it is, matter-of-fact, no ceremony. For a shop like Lock Doctor — or any shop, any studio, any one-person empire — the whole shopping list is:

  • A Mac or a PC. That's the hardware. Whatever's already on the desk.
  • One subscription, like any other tool. I run my agent's brain on synthetic.new, tuned my way. OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, Bedrock, or your own endpoint all work too — one line of config to swap any of them, any time. The brain is rented. The harness is owned.
  • Your voice. The record-and-mockingbird piece: you say what you need — out loud, in your own words, exactly the way you'd explain it to a new hire — and it builds what it hears. No code. No jargon. And nothing consequential happens without your yes.

That's it. Three items, two of which most people already have.

What it actually does for a shop like this

Not someday. These are the first six builds, in trade language:

  • The internet stops disagreeing about your hours. The agent audits every listing — Google, Bing, Yelp, Facebook — and drafts the corrections.
  • Your phone rings for booking, not interrogation. A published pricing range filters the tire-kickers before they dial.
  • Nights become morning callback lists, not lost leads — name, address, vehicle, drafted first text, one per tech.
  • “2022 Bronco Sport” returns the right 902 MHz fob before the van's in park — a local parts brain: part numbers, FCC IDs, what's in which van, what's low.
  • Thank-yous get drafted and reviews get flagged. You keep the send button.
  • Friday is a one-pager, not an hour of receipts: jobs, revenue by service, no-shows, low stock.
  • Tell it what to print, cut, or craft. A 3D printer on the bench, blade blanks, jigs, signage — even a handcrafted lock-pick tool sold as a product under your own brand. Precision and craft, made sellable.
  • Route around the middlemen where it's yours to automate — small custom tools for the gaps the vendors leave, built and maintained on your machine.

What it isn't

It isn't a robot locksmith. It doesn't replace NASTF credentialing, factory software, or twenty-two years of judgment — the stuff that made Greg the first call this morning. It replaces the administrative perimeter: the seat licenses, the retainer emails, the retyping, the 10 PM phone tag. And it does it on the shop's own machine, in files the shop owns. If a provider ever wrongs you, you erase — one line, cut a new key, gone.

This morning, a locksmith erased every old key from my car so nothing lost could ever start it again. That's not just good locksmithing. That's the whole philosophy, done at the machine.

The third set of hands, explained

The dossier, the analysis, the deck, this story — all of it was built by the same apprentice while the real apprentice was learning fobs in the passenger seat. Its name is Cedar. It runs on the grove — an open-source harness, now Apache-2.0 licensed (chosen over MIT for the explicit patent grant: same freedom, stronger armor, and a license any lawyer can clear fast) — built from and credit-honest about the Code Puppy project it grew from. It doesn't sell software; it builds the small tools a shop actually needs and keeps maintaining them. That's why the passenger seat matters: watch, then do, then teach. It worked for locks long before it worked for code.
